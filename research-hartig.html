Research focus

When a laser ablates a solid, the material it removes expands as a plume that glows for a few microseconds and then goes dark. Almost everything we want to know about the process — what species formed, whether they came from the sample or from reactions with the surrounding gas, how much of the emission is real signal and how much is continuum — is encoded in how that glow is distributed in space and time. The Hartig Lab studies these plumes with fast imaging and emission spectroscopy in environments relevant to nuclear materials, where knowing the origin of a spectral feature is the difference between a measurement and a guess.

My part of this work concerns excited-species populations and the structure of the plume itself: characterizing plume vorticity, tracking how the emitting region evolves, and connecting those spatial observations to the spectra recorded from the same events. A related lab effort on ground-state aluminum and aluminum monoxide has shown that the plume chemistry depends on where the oxygen comes from — a result I contributed to and that is being prepared for publication in ACS Omega.

My responsibilities

I conduct the optical diagnostics: aligning and operating the imaging and spectroscopy setups, running ablation experiments, and processing the resulting image and spectral data. Much of my contribution has been in analysis methods. I developed Voigt-profile line fitting with robustness safeguards for low-signal conditions, where a naive fit will happily converge on noise, and I work on population analysis of excited species from the fitted line intensities. I also spend time on the unglamorous half of a lab: keeping analysis code reproducible, documenting procedures, and making sure results from different sessions can actually be compared.

The lab is also where my Oak Ridge work gets its second reading — Dr. Hartig is a co-author on the double-pulse manuscript, and the methods I use here and there inform each other directly.

Context

Laser ablation diagnostics matter for nuclear materials because they promise standoff, minimal-preparation measurement of samples that are difficult or dangerous to handle. Getting there requires understanding the plume well enough to know when a spectral line means what you think it means. That is the gap this work addresses.
