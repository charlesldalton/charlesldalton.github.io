Research focus

Plastics do not disappear; they fragment, weather, and redistribute. Characterizing that process requires identifying which polymers are present in a sample and how far degradation has proceeded, which is a chemical fingerprinting problem. The Jung Lab used gas chromatography–mass spectrometry to make those identifications on both manufactured plastics and environmental samples, including a campus-wide survey of how microplastic composition varies by location at the University of Florida.

My responsibilities

I performed GC–MS experimentation on manufactured plastics and environmental samples, characterizing polymer degradation products and microplastic composition. That covered sample preparation, running the instrument, and working through the resulting chromatograms to assign peaks. I co-authored the campus microplastic survey presented at the UF Undergraduate Research Symposium in April 2024.

This was my first research position, starting in my second semester at Florida, and it is where I learned the basic grammar of experimental work: blanks, controls, replicates, and the discipline of not believing a peak until you have checked it.

Context

The subject matter is far from reactors, but the skills transferred directly. Every diagnostic technique I have used since — LIBS, UV–Vis, emission imaging — is the same problem in a different medium: a spectrum comes back, and the work is deciding what in it is real.
