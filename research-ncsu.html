Research focus

Molten chloride salts are a leading candidate coolant and fuel carrier for advanced reactors, and their optical properties carry chemical information — oxidation states, corrosion products, dissolved species — that is otherwise hard to obtain without removing and quenching a sample. Quenching changes the thing you wanted to measure. The alternative is to measure the salt while it is molten, which requires a cell that holds a corrosive melt above 500 °C, keeps oxygen and water out, and still lets ultraviolet, visible, and terahertz light pass cleanly through it.

This project, part of a NEUP-funded effort on ultrafast spectroscopy of molten salts, built that cell: a Sample Analysis by Light Transmission (SALT) furnace designed for both UV–Vis absorbance and terahertz time-domain spectroscopy of the same sample.

My responsibilities

I designed and built the furnace: CAD design of the body and windowed sample cell, selection of materials compatible with molten LiCl–KCl, and assembly and commissioning of the heating and inert-atmosphere systems. I then configured the optical path — source, focusing optics, and detector alignment through the cell — and used the finished instrument to collect UV–Vis spectra of the molten eutectic. I presented the furnace design and the associated measurement method at the NEUP annual review meeting in Raleigh in July 2025.

This was my first project where I owned a piece of hardware from a blank CAD file through to data, and it changed how I think about experiments: the design decisions made in week two set the noise floor in week ten.

Context

The wider effort aims to use ultrafast and broadband spectroscopy to understand molten salt structure at a fundamental level, feeding models that reactor designers depend on. A transmission cell is unglamorous infrastructure, but every optical measurement of a melt is limited by it.
